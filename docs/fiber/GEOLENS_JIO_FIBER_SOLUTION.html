1. Executive summary

Jio Fiber operations teams need a single, trusted view of how the network is performing for customers, where it is failing right now, and where the field force should go next. GeoLens provides that view as an interactive map built on the Databricks Data Intelligence Platform, in the spirit of commercial location-intelligence tools, but running entirely on the customer's own lakehouse data and governance.

  • See the whole network at a glance. H3 hexagons colour every area of every metro by a 0 to 100 network health score built from uptime, optical margin, latency, loss, throughput and trouble tickets. Fixed operational bands keep healthy areas calm and make real problems stand out.
  • Act on live incidents. Open incidents appear on the map from the first screen, coloured by severity. Critical (P1) incidents keep glowing until they are resolved. One click shows impact, root cause and the nearest available technicians, and dispatches one.
  • Drill without losing context. Click any hexagon to zoom into exactly that area, then keep clicking down to street level. A breadcrumb and a Back button return you to where you were.
  • Build views with drag and drop. A data catalog of 17 layers (incidents, technicians, health, uptime, optical power, capacity, growth and cross-sell) can be dragged onto the map or into widgets.
  • Governed by design. Region and metro entitlements are defined once in Unity Catalog and enforced both by Unity Catalog row filters and by Postgres row-level security in Lakebase. A Delhi NOC lead only ever sees Delhi.
  • Ask in plain language. An AI assistant built on Databricks Foundation Model APIs answers questions such as "which OLTs will run out of capacity first?" and moves the map to show the answer.

2. The operational challenge

A home broadband network at Jio's scale generates signals from many systems: network management alarms, ONT optical telemetry, OLT port utilisation, trouble tickets, workforce positions and the subscriber base. Each team typically sees one slice in one tool. The questions the NOC and regional leadership actually ask are spatial and cross-cutting:

PersonaQuestions they need answered in seconds
NOC leadWhat is broken right now, how many homes are affected, which incidents are critical, and who is the nearest available technician?
Regional headWhich localities in my region have degrading customer experience this week, and is it optical, capacity or field related?
Capacity plannerWhich OLTs are exhausted or will exhaust within six months, and where should we augment or split PON ports?
Field operationsWhere are my technicians, who is free, and are dispatches matching incident severity?
Growth and salesWhere are homes passed but not connected, where is premium plan mix low, and where are Reliance Retail customers who do not yet have Jio Fiber?

The solution therefore had to meet five requirements that were set at the start:

  • Hexagon maps of network KPIs at multiple resolutions, from national down to street level.
  • Fast responses for thousands of concurrent users, so the serving layer must be an operational database (Lakebase), not a warehouse.
  • Live updates: the map must refresh by itself as new data lands, with no page reload.
  • Region-scoped access managed centrally through Unity Catalog and Postgres row-level security.
  • Drag and drop: adding information to the map should not require code or SQL.

Realism built into the data

  • Thirty days of history (10 September to 9 October 2026) with daily and hourly patterns, so trends, weekly comparisons and sparklines behave like production data.
  • Network vintage matters. Each OLT area has a build year. Older areas show weaker optical margins and more faults, which is how real fiber estates age.
  • Named stress events make the story visible: a three-day cyclone in Chennai drives a cluster of power failures and cable cuts, and construction activity in Gurugram drives repeated distribution cuts.
  • Correlated signals. Cell-level noise is blended with OLT-level effects, so a failing OLT degrades every hexagon it serves, as in a real PON tree.
  • Capacity pressure is concentrated in dense localities, producing exhausted OLTs and "augment within six months" candidates for the planner.
  • Live operations. In this environment a simulator inside the app raises new incidents every few seconds, moves technicians towards jobs and resolves incidents. In production the same tables are fed by the network management system, OSS alarms and the workforce management system.
All data in this document is synthetic and was created for the demonstration. Table structures, grain and metric definitions are designed to map directly to Jio's real NMS, OSS, BSS and workforce sources.

Key design choices

ChoiceWhy
H3 hexagons at resolutions 5 to 9Equal-area cells that aggregate cleanly. The map picks a resolution from the zoom level and requests fixed tiles of at most 343 cells, so every request is small and cacheable.
Lakebase as the serving layerMap interactions need single-digit to tens of milliseconds per request and thousands of concurrent users. Lakebase gives Postgres latency and concurrency, with tables synced from Unity Catalog instead of hand-built ETL.
Read replica for map trafficTile and point reads go to a read-only endpoint; writes (dispatches, saved views) go to the primary.
PostGIS vector tilesFiber routes are line geometry. PostGIS renders them as vector tiles on demand, so tens of thousands of cable segments stay fast.
Push, not pollPostgres notifications drive server-sent events to every open browser. Only the layers whose data changed are refetched.
One entitlement modelThe same region and metro scope table drives Unity Catalog row filters for analysts and Postgres row-level security for app users.

6. Security and access control

Access is defined once, in a Unity Catalog table that maps each person or role to a scope: national, one or more regions, or one or more metros. That single definition is enforced in two places.

  • Unity Catalog row filters on all 17 gold tables. Analysts using SQL, notebooks, dashboards or Genie only see rows in their scope.
  • Postgres row-level security in Lakebase. The app reads through security-barrier views that apply the same scope to every query. The scope is resolved once per query, so enforcement adds no measurable latency.
  • Least privilege for the app. The app's identity can read only the secured views and write only to its own operational tables. It cannot bypass row-level security.
  • Workspace single sign-on. Users reach the app with their Databricks identity. Presenters can switch to demonstration personas to show what each role sees.

7. The storyline: a day in the NOC

This walkthrough follows a NOC lead through a typical session. Every screenshot was captured from the deployed app during the end-to-end test run described in section 9.

1Land on what matters: live incidents

The control center opens on the national view with only live incidents switched on, so nothing distracts from what is broken right now. Each dot is an open incident coloured by severity. Critical P1 incidents glow continuously until they are fixed. The KPI strip shows homes connected, network health, open incidents, homes impacted, field force and capacity at a glance. Network health and technicians are already in the layer list, switched off, ready when needed.

2Watch the live feed and triage

The live feed lists the newest incidents as they arrive. Clicking one flies the map to it and opens the incident panel: type, severity, homes impacted, start time, root cause and the five nearest available technicians with their skills and distance.

3Dispatch in one click

Dispatch assigns the chosen technician. The assignment is written to Lakebase, every connected screen is notified, and the technician starts moving towards the job on the map.

4Turn on network health across India

Ticking Network health score adds customer experience to the map. At national zoom, cells are rolled up into larger hexagons so each metro stays readable. Colours use fixed operational bands (red below 60, blue above 90), so a red area means a real problem, not just the worst of a good set.

5Drill into a hexagon without losing your place

Clicking a hexagon zooms to exactly that area and shows only the detail inside it. A breadcrumb records the path, and Back returns to the previous view.

6Focus on one metro

Choosing a metro in the top bar restricts everything on screen to that metro: map, KPIs, widgets, live feed and the AI assistant. The histogram and top-cells widgets recompute for the area in view, and a click on any bar filters the map.

7Ask GeoLens

The assistant answers operational questions in plain language. It queries the same governed data the map uses, respects the user's scope and the selected metro, and acts on the map: it adds layers, flies to places, highlights cells and opens the relevant OLT.

8Build your own view with drag and drop

The data catalog on the left groups 17 layers into Operations, Experience, Growth and Network. Users drag a layer onto the map or into the widget rail, reorder layers by dragging, change the metric, colour and size, and save the view to share with colleagues. No SQL or code is needed.

8. Always current: data freshness

Two kinds of change reach the map automatically, with no page reload.

  • Live operations (new incidents, dispatches, technician movement, resolutions) are written to Lakebase and pushed to every open browser within seconds through Postgres notifications and server-sent events.
  • Analytical refreshes (new daily KPIs, updated OLT inventory, new incidents in the lakehouse) arrive through synced tables. The app detects the new table version, increases the dataset version and tells every open map to refetch only the affected layers.

Most of this time is the synced-table refresh itself. Refreshes can be scheduled or triggered when the upstream gold table changes; live operational data does not wait for them.

10. Scale and performance

Load tests ran from a Databricks serverless job in the same cloud region as the app, simulating map users who pan, zoom and refresh with HTTP/2 connections, as browsers do.

ScenarioThroughputErrorsServer time per tile
150 users, no think time (saturation)299 req/s0p50 12.8 ms · p95 35 ms
1,000 users on 1 app instance206 req/s0.07%p50 18 ms
1,000 users on 4 app instances182 req/sconnection limitsp50 24 ms
  • The data layer is not the bottleneck. Lakebase and the app answer each map tile in tens of milliseconds even under saturation, thanks to tile-sized queries, a shared cache with version-aware keys, ETag revalidation and the read replica.
  • Single-source load tests hit an ingress limit. All simulated users came from one machine and one identity. Throughput levelled off at 200 to 300 requests per second regardless of instance count, and most client-side wait happened before requests reached the app. Real users arrive from many devices and identities and are not limited this way.
  • Validating thousands of real users calls for a distributed, multi-identity test. The architecture scales horizontally on both tiers: more app instances behind the same URL, and more Lakebase read replicas for map traffic.

Why it stays fast

  • Each map request covers one fixed tile of at most 343 hexagons, so query cost is bounded regardless of zoom.
  • Entitlement scope is evaluated once per query instead of once per row.
  • Responses are cached per scope and dataset version. When data changes, the version changes and stale entries are never served.
  • Browsers revalidate with ETags, so unchanged tiles return in a few hundred bytes.

Appendix D. Basemap: removing the CARTO dependency

GeoLens was built to run entirely on Databricks and open-source software. In the current build there is one remaining third-party dependency: the background basemap is loaded from CARTO's public basemap service. This appendix explains exactly what that dependency covers and how it is removed.

What depends on CARTO today

ItemTodayEffect
Basemap stylesDark Matter (Jio Fiber view) and Positron (light view), loaded from basemaps.cartocdn.comThe grey or dark background map: roads, water, land use, place names and borders
Basemap tilesVector tiles served by CARTO's content delivery networkEach user's browser downloads background tiles directly from CARTO
Fonts and map iconsLabel fonts and sprites referenced by the CARTO stylesUsed to draw place names on the background map
Attribution"© CARTO, © OpenStreetMap contributors" shown on the mapRequired while CARTO's service is used

What does not depend on CARTO

  • All Jio Fiber data, hexagons, incidents, technicians, OLTs, fiber routes, KPIs and widgets, which are served from Lakebase by the GeoLens app.
  • Security, entitlements, live updates and the AI assistant, which run on Unity Catalog, Lakebase, Databricks Apps and Foundation Model APIs.
  • The map software itself: deck.gl, MapLibre GL and H3 are open source and need no CARTO account, key, SDK or licence.

No CARTO account, API key, software development kit or paid service is used anywhere in GeoLens.

Why remove it

  • Licensing. CARTO's free basemaps are intended for limited use. Production use at Jio scale would require a commercial agreement.
  • Network access. Users' browsers must reach an external service on the internet, which may not be allowed inside a restricted enterprise network.
  • Availability and control. The look and uptime of the background map depend on a third party.

The Dark Matter and Positron styles are themselves open source, published through the OpenMapTiles project. Keeping them preserves the look users already know, while the tiles, fonts and icons move inside the Databricks environment.

How the dependency is removed

StepWhat happens
1. Build the basemapGenerate OpenStreetMap vector tiles in the OpenMapTiles schema for India, with full street detail for the served metros, using the open-source Planetiler tool on Databricks serverless compute.
2. Store it in DatabricksKeep the tile archive in a Unity Catalog Volume and load the tiles into Lakebase, so the basemap is served the same governed, low-latency way as the network data.
3. Serve it from GeoLensAdd a basemap tile endpoint to the GeoLens app with caching and revalidation, and bundle the open label fonts and map icons with the application.
4. Switch the mapPoint the map engine at self-hosted copies of the Dark Matter and Positron styles and update the on-map attribution.
5. VerifyRerun the end-to-end storyline and confirm the browser makes no requests to any external map service.
6. Keep it currentRebuild the basemap on a schedule with Lakeflow Jobs to pick up new roads and places from OpenStreetMap.

After the change

  • No CARTO service, content delivery network, account or licence is used.
  • The application works inside a closed network: every map request goes to the GeoLens app URL.
  • The map keeps the same look and feel, with small differences in label density at some zoom levels.
  • The only remaining obligation is attribution on the map: "© OpenMapTiles © OpenStreetMap contributors", with credit for the open style design as its licence requires.
